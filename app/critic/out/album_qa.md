## Album Review

If "I'm Still Here" was Rose taking honest stock and "Outlandia" was the two-hour civic autopsy, "Wazoo In Effigy" is the sound of the satirist deciding he'd rather run eight tight sketches than one long panorama, and mostly it works because the comedy is welded to the craft rather than hung on it. "Knurled Spanner" opens the door with a barker's address to "Gentlemen, homeowners, debtors, sufferers" and a chorus that punches on the title like a brass hit, and it declares the whole enterprise in a breath: absurdist premises, crooked meters that never let you settle, and a closing knife twist ("You still don't need it, and you never did") that turns the joke into a verdict. From there Rose keeps the voice consistent even as the palette wanders, from the propulsive self-permission of "Spontaneous Fugue" to the cheerful civic horror of "Municipal Tongue Clamp," whose choir sings "Clamp it down, citizen, nice and snug" before the dread registers.

The record earns its shape in the back half. "Hey Boob Shirley" and "Free To Pee" run the same trick, a grown man dressing base instinct in social theory, and stacking them risks fatigue, but they're the setup for the album's real peak: "Message From Bob" builds a whole theology out of an old friend's misdialed voicemails and means it, that single telephone ring near the five-minute mark landing the best-placed silence here since "You Don't Say." It's the hinge the album turns on, funny and genuinely argued at once, and Rose was right to put it where the ear is fully warmed up. "Waltzing Tilde," the one where two people insist they're the same and language quietly proves them wrong, asks the most patience and gives up its architecture slowest; placed just after the record's emotional high, it costs a little momentum, though "So we went to the same sign" collapsing into "The pronoun" rewards the listener who stays. The title track closes by putting the critic himself in the dock, an electric orchestra as courtroom, and the third-verse turn ("Three hundred dollars to make both the records; ten thousand later for hired-in men") complicates the target enough that the flat, bored "Talentless" cutting the trumpet dead feels earned rather than smug.

This is a varied set held together by a single sensibility: Randy Newman's unreliable narrators run through a prog-jazz ensemble that treats displaced accents and abrupt stops as punchlines. It doesn't cohere the way a diarist's record does, and the two farces in a row plus the demanding waltz mean it plays best across a couple of spins rather than one. But the sequencing knows what it has, saving its warmest and its most daring for the last three, and the whole lands as a confident, cohesive statement of who Rose is when he lets the joke carry real weight. A confident record that holds together.

## Contextual Track Reviews

### [1] knurled-spanner  _standalone 4_

The record opens the way a con man opens a pitch, and Rose the satirist is in full flower here, selling you a tool that does nothing and knowing you'll buy it anyway. The whole thing turns on the mock-infomercial cadence: a barker's opening address to "Gentlemen, homeowners, debtors, sufferers" gives way to a chorus that punches on the title like a brass hit, all riding an electric-piano ostinato in a driving 3/4 that never once lets you waltz. The craft is in the details, and Rose stacks them: Mrs. Vale and her "three sensible lampshades," the deadpan post-chorus muttering "No warranty on the mood," and the show-stealing bridge where he addresses the critics head-on, insisting they've "confused 'tool' with 'permission.'" Best of all is the arrangement's cruelty toward its own hype: when the drums stop cold before "That's where most people get it wrong," then the massive brass swells back, you feel the joke land in your chest. The wiry guitar solo, marked to repeat unchanged, is the punchline made instrumental, a thing looking busy while doing very little. If there's a caution, it's that the concept is so airtight that the fourth pass of the hook offers diminishing returns; this is a piece that rewards attention more than the shuffle button, and it sets the terms for everything the album does after it. But the closing gut-punch, "You still don't need it, and you never did," earns the whole runtime, and puts you on notice that this record intends to argue with you. One of the sharpest things he's built since Outlandia.

### [2] spontaneous-fugue  _standalone 3_

After the airtight con of "Knurled Spanner," this is the album exhaling, and the shift in register does it good. The title promises something knottier than what lands, and that gap is the story of this one: an art-rock brief built on shifting meters and clipped sax multiphonics that resolves, in the audio, into a bright and propulsive E-major push at a mid-tempo clip. What survives the translation is Rose's gift for the small confessional image. The best verse puts you on the commute directly: 'On the 6:12 with my bag half-zipped / Listening to the brakes sigh at the stop,' and that sighing brake does more work than any angular riff could. The chorus turns on a nice piece of self-permission, 'If it's a detour, let it be / I'm moving, I'm moving,' and the guitar solo near the four-minute mark gives the final chorus a genuine lift, the same forward tilt Rose reached for on 'Nowhere But Up.' It doesn't earn its experimental framing so much as sidestep it into a clean, hook-forward anthem, and the 'rearranging the furniture' verse leans a touch on the nose next to that train-window sharpness. Placed here, it's the steadying breath before the satire tightens its grip again; the writing keeps its footing and the momentum never sags. A dependable cut that does its job and does it well.

### [3] municipal-tongue-clamp  _standalone 4_

If "Spontaneous Fugue" was the album catching its breath, this is Rose walking back into the municipal building, and after the two-hour civic autopsy of "Outlandia" he trades the panorama for a single grotesque department, and the compression suits him. The whole thing runs on the joke of that chorus: an absurdly cheerful civic choir singing "Clamp it down, citizen, nice and snug" over a bright electric-piano bounce, the kind of hook you catch yourself humming before the horror registers. Built in F minor at a shuffling clip, it lets the crooked meters do the satire, the way the band chokes to silence right before "and fits you for a tongue clamp," or the furious contrapuntal break where guitar, sax, and marimba chase the same angular figure through shifting bars without ever tipping into chaos. The bridge is the payoff, a sinister doo-wop sweetness under the moment Eddie gets cut off mid-sentence: "Your silence has been entered into the permanent record," delivered with the pleasantness of a parking clerk. Then the breakdown drops the mask and lets Rose sound genuinely incredulous: "nobody knew who ordered the clamp." If it has a weakness, it is length; at nearly six minutes the instructional interludes start to feel like they are proving a point already made. But the tiny lounge-combo outro, that final "Open wide. Perfect," lands the knife clean. Closest cousin might be Randy Newman writing for a Kafka pageant, and it starts the run of character comedy the album's middle lives on.

### [4] hey-boob-shirley  _standalone 4_

Where "Municipal Tongue Clamp" turned its satire on institutions, this one turns it on a single sweaty man, and the comedy here isn't in spite of the craft, it's built from it. Rose sets a marimba walking figure loose over a broken-waltz pulse at a brisk clip, and the arrangement earns its jokes with timing: the stop-start accents on 'Peripheral. Accidental. Purely educational' land like a man caught red-handed, and the whole thing pivots on that guilty deadpan of a grown man who'll 'study grout / Like what he noticed four feet before.' The mock-anthropology of verse two ('First you cover for the weather. Then you decorate the hide') is where the writing sharpens into something Randy Newman would recognize, an unreliable narrator dressing up base instinct as social theory. The 5/4 instrumental break is the record's real flex, marimba restating the motif in a 3+2 stagger while the flute exaggerates the strut and the guitar answers crooked, before the final bar snaps abruptly back to a straight-4/4 chorus. If the bit runs slightly long, the outro's catalog of 'feathers, beads, buckles, buttons' testing your patience before the final 'Nice grout, by the way' pays it off, the way a good closing line pays off a shaggy joke. Proof Rose can make a novelty conceit hold real musical weight, and one more voice in the album's gallery of men caught mid-excuse.

### [5] free-to-pee  _standalone 4_

This is the third character sketch in a row, and by now you can feel the album's central comic engine humming: here is Rose the comic who has been hiding inside Rose the diarist all along, and "Free To Pee" is a full-blown one-act farce set to a brisk 3/4 that never once invites you to waltz. The whole thing hangs on the tension between Ted's escalating legal defense and Linda, whose entire vocabulary is a barked "TED!" landing on a sax-and-synth stab: the negotiation, the physics, the improved trajectory, all delivered with the wounded dignity of a man arguing before a court that does not exist. The doo-wop chorus turning martial by the second pass is the joke doing double duty, and the bridge is where it earns its keep, when the band drops to organ and bass and Ted goes philosopher: "That's water, patience, gravity, the original authority," then "Fifty-eight has humbled better men than me." There is real craft in how the outro cuts everything dead and loops back to that opening "Damn. Be right back," a punchline that reframes the whole five minutes as a bit Ted has clearly run before. It is broad, and coming after two comparably staged farces a few of the stop-start verses run longer than the gag strictly needs, so this is one for the room rather than the headphones; but as a piece of theater it holds the floor and knows exactly what it is.

### [6] message-from-bob  _standalone 5_

After a run of pure character farce, Rose lets the joke turn into something you can actually believe in, and this is the album's high-water mark. He has always found a lifetime in the ordinary, but here he goes further: he builds a whole theology out of an old friend's rambling voicemails. Over a dry, syncopated Rhodes-and-bass vamp that never quite settles, at a steady clip in a bright major key, the deadpan spoken vocal delivers Bob's misdialed apologies and glued-shut windows as if they were scripture, then the band snaps into a harmonized hook: "A message from Bob / is a message from God / if everything is one. / That's not religion. / That's arithmetic." The joke keeps sharpening into something closer to a real argument, and the bridge's inventory of red lights and falling ice cubes lands the thesis cold: "Meaning is everywhere / once you lower the standard." A single telephone ring near the five-minute mark triggers a hard cut that's the best-placed silence on the record since "You Don't Say," and the fuzz-guitar answers between verses keep the whole thing from tipping into pure sketch. It runs long, and the fourth verse leans more on concept than melody, but this is Rose at his most gleefully committed to a bit that turns out to mean it. Where the comedy stops being a costume and becomes a conviction. Play this one first.

### [7] waltzing-tilde  _standalone 2_

Coming off the committed sprawl of "Message From Bob," this is the album's most rigorous turn inward, and the whole record hangs on a single joke that Rose refuses to let be funny: two people insisting they're the same, and language quietly proving them wrong every time. It begins with a three-note figure, a rising minor third and a falling semitone, stated in plain octave unison by piano and marimba, and the entire piece is that motif under stress: reversed, inverted, stacked in different meters until the players stop agreeing on where the bar starts. Listen to the third interlude, where guitar walks off in 5/4 while the piano keeps its waltz and the bar lines quietly stop coinciding; by the closing section the ensemble strains toward a 7/8 stumble before snapping back to one last unison, with the marimba holding a single wrong pitch like a held grudge. The dialogue stays near-spoken and dry, the alto landing the killers: 'So we went to the same sign,' and then the whole argument collapsing into 'The pronoun.' It asks patience, and its pleasures are more architectural than immediate, so it plays as a grower more than a first-spin favorite; but for anyone who followed the meter games of 'Unreasonably Quiet' this far, it's a fascinating, rigorous thing, and it earns its late-album placement as the moment the record stops performing and starts thinking. Worthy of a listen.

### [8] wazoo-in-effigy  _standalone 4_

The title track waits until the very end, and it makes you glad it did. Rose has spent 2026 pivoting hard, from the diarist of 'I'm Still Here' to the political sprawl of 'Outlandia,' but nothing in the catalog quite prepares you for this: a full electric orchestra deployed as courtroom, with the critic himself in the dock. The marimba pulse and fragmented brass stabs open in a jittery 7/8 before the whole thing lurches into a 4/4 chorus at a driving clip, and the tension between those meters is the point, an ensemble that keeps snapping shut on itself, abrupt stops leaving the baritone exposed. The genius here is structural: the song reads like a demolition of a man ('Technique, certainly. Feeling? I doubt it') until the third verse quietly turns the knife, 'Three hundred dollars to make both the records; ten thousand later for hired-in men,' and you realize the target may deserve some of this and none of it. Then the bridge lands the real gut-punch, the same wild-side hero who called Wazoo 'the single most untalented person I've heard in my life' standing at a podium twenty years later to induct him, and Rose's flat refrain, 'No correction was printed. Corrections are not printed,' says everything about who actually holds power in the room. After an album that put the critic's dismissals in a con man's mouth back on "Knurled Spanner," this closer settles the score directly, and the closing trumpet solo tears through the shifting meters before that dry, bored spoken 'Talentless' cuts it dead, an ending that would feel like a stunt if it hadn't been earned across everything before it. It asks a lot of a listener, and the density means it reveals itself over several passes, but the craft is unmistakable. One of the most daring things he's put his name to.

## Album Review

Rose spent the spring alternating between the diarist who finds a lifetime in a phone reflection and the sprawling architect of "Outlandia," and "Wazoo In Effigy" answers both by picking a third door entirely: an eight-track homage to Frank Zappa, built in odd meters and delivered almost entirely in that dry baritone talk-sing that treats a satire like a legal deposition. "Knurled Spanner" declares the terms right away, a late-night infomercial run through a jazz-fusion band that won't sit still, and the whole record keeps that posture: everything measured, nothing smoothed, the joke always sitting inside the arrangement rather than on top of it. This is a varied palette held together by attitude more than sound, the electric-piano ostinatos and marimba walking-motifs and abrupt classical-ensemble cuts recurring often enough to feel like a house style. It plays like a comic songbook that keeps deepening past the punchline, and it works because Rose never lets the bit off the leash.

The middle stretch is where it earns its keep. "Municipal Tongue Clamp" is the peak, a single absurd ordinance zoomed in until the cheerful civic choir chirping "Clamp it down, citizen, nice and snug" turns genuinely cold in the doo-wop bridge, and it sits at the record's center like a keystone. From there "Hey Boob Shirley," "Free To Pee," and "Message From Bob" run a remarkable streak of comic set-pieces that never repeat a trick: the marimba miming a walk across a room, the mock-courtroom scrambling around Linda's off-mic "TED!", and Bob's misdialed voicemails building toward "Meaning is everywhere / once you lower the standard." The two quieter, more cerebral cuts, "Spontaneous Fugue" and the fracturing waltz of "Waltzing Tilde," ask more patience than they immediately return; "Fugue" leans on its chorus more than it reinvents, and "Tilde" lives so completely in its design that the payoff is the marimba holding one wrong note rather than any hook. Neither costs the album much, but they are the moments where the faithful lean in and the newcomer might drift.

"Wazoo In Effigy" closes exactly where it should, because it's the argument the whole record has been circling: Rose takes on Zappa's critics directly, letting the sneering "wild side hero" who calls Wazoo "the single most untalented person I've heard in my life" induct him into the Hall twenty years later with no correction printed. It's the Randy Newman trick of handing a monster the microphone, and the deliberate "Talentless" aftertaste leaves you cold on purpose, a closer built to be unpacked rather than hummed. This is the rare case where the sequence lifts the parts: the two soft-floor tracks would read thinner in isolation, but placed as the breathers between the comic peaks they give the record its shape, and the whole thing lands as a unified statement of intent. Not the easiest record in his catalog, and more built than felt in places, but it's a confident, cohesive one, and it holds the room.

## Contextual Track Reviews

### [1] knurled-spanner  _standalone 3_

The record opens like a late-night infomercial run through a jazz-fusion band that refuses to sit still: an electric-piano ostinato loops at a brisk clip while clipped brass and two guitars trade angular jabs, and Rose talk-sings the whole con with a straight face. As a first handshake it sets the terms for everything that follows, the dry wit, the odd meters, the satire built from a single absurd object. The joke is right there in the spanner itself, a tool whose knurling is 'mostly there so your hand feels employed while the thing itself does very little,' and Rose builds a whole indictment of useless progress on that one image. Mrs. Vale on Sycamore Close gets her verse and her ruined tumble dryer; the mock-critic bridge lands one of the album's sharpest lines when it accuses us of confusing 'tool' with 'permission.' The deadpan post-chorus, those flat '(Probably help.)' backing vocals with 'no warranty on the mood,' rewards a second pass. It runs a touch long across its five minutes, and the wiry guitar solo repeats its figure a beat past where you want it to move on, so this reads as an entry for listeners already tuned to Rose's humor rather than a crowd-pleaser up front. But the closing turn, the ostinato left alone and that final 'You still don't need it, and you never did,' gives the gag a real spine. A grower, and a smart way to raise the curtain.

### [2] spontaneous-fugue  _standalone 2_

After the opener's angular pitch, Rose pulls the tempo back to a walking pace and turns inward, though the meter stays every bit as restless: settled but shifting underneath, guitar and fretless bass interlocking and then peeling apart at each of the dozen section seams. The title promises a runaway, and the track treats meter like a moving vehicle. The best writing here is the third verse, where the philosophy comes down to earth: 'On the 6:12 with my bag half-zipped, / Listening to the brakes sigh at the stop.' That's the whole song in two lines, the commuter's quiet reckoning about whether he chose the route or the route chose him, and it lands harder than the more abstract talk of being 'aligned with the cosmos' earlier on. His phrasing stays tightly measured, uniform line lengths clipped to the beat, so when the bridge loosens into 'Maybe the map is all a rumor,' you feel the rail let go for a second. It runs long at four and a half minutes and the chorus leans on repetition more than reinvention, so this one is for the faithful, the listeners who've followed Rose from the diary songs through the angular experiments. But there's real craft in how the stop-start edits keep undercutting the groove without ever losing it. A grower once you stop looking for the map, and the quiet before the record's satirical heart opens up.

### [3] municipal-tongue-clamp  _standalone 4_

This is where the record finds its center of gravity. Two tracks in, Rose zooms his lens down to a single absurd ordinance and holds it there until it's monstrous. He builds it at a laid-back pace in a minor key, then keeps knocking the floor out from under you: the crooked figures in the verses, the full-band choke that swallows the line before 'you're looking at an auxiliary clamp,' the way straight 4/4 keeps getting sabotaged by odd bars in the chorus so you never quite settle. The genius is in the delivery, that dry baritone talk-sing reading bureaucratic nonsense with a straight face while a cheerful civic choir chirps 'Clamp it down, citizen, nice and snug' like it's a jingle you're meant to whistle. The doo-wop bridge is where the joke turns cold: Eddie starts to say the property tax 'seemed,' a metallic snap cuts him off, and the sweet backing hum that answers is the most sinister thing on the record. It runs close to six minutes and the mid-section instrumental break, all angular theme traded around in shifting meter, asks patience of anyone who came for the hook. But the payoff, 'Praise be unto the Municipal Tongue Clamp' over a grotesque pageant that collapses into a tiny lounge combo cooing 'Perfect,' earns it. Randy Newman's satirical bite crossed with the odd-meter mischief of prog-jazz, this is the strongest thing here, and it holds the room.

### [4] hey-boob-shirley  _standalone 4_

Coming off the cold civic pageant of the last track, Rose lightens the touch without loosening the craft, and the album settles into a run of comic set-pieces that all pull their weight. The joke here is the arrangement itself: a marimba that opens by miming a woman walking across a room, then keeps that walking motif running through the whole thing like a straight face at a comedy roast. Rose plays anthropologist, dry and deadpan, delivering the verses in a talk-sung mumble that treats a man's caught glance as field research: 'Nothing makes a grown man study grout / Like what he noticed four feet before.' The chorus hook is genuinely sticky, and the brisk pulse keeps everything slightly off its heels, the broken-waltz bridge tipping into that 'three beats for displaying, two beats for the scan' bit that lands the mock-science joke perfectly. What sounds like a minor key gives the whole thing a wry, unsmiling edge rather than a wink. It runs long at five-plus minutes and the spoken outro pushes the conceit a beat past where a tighter edit would have left it, but the payoff line, 'Nice grout, by the way,' earns the wait. Closest cousin might be Frank Zappa in a lecturing mood, or They Might Be Giants writing a nature documentary. For a comic set-piece, it's built with real care, and it lets you laugh right when the record needs you to.

### [5] free-to-pee  _standalone 4_

Sitting dead center in the record's comic stretch, this is Rose at his most audacious: a full five-minute argument built around a man who can't find a legal place to relieve himself, and the nerve is half the fun. It opens exactly where it ends, with a muttered 'Damn. Be right back,' and in between he runs a full mock-courtroom at a scrambling clip, the electric-piano ostinato and clipped horn stabs interrupted every few bars by Linda's off-mic one-word verdicts: 'TED!' 'NO!' 'WHAT?!' The best writing here is the mock-legal register played dead straight, 'the pavement didn't file a plea,' 'nature bangs the gavel,' 'nature has seniority,' delivered with the wounded dignity of a man who genuinely believes he's been wronged. The bridge is where it turns from bit to song: the band drops to organ and bass, and Rose lands his one true confession, 'Fifty-eight has humbled better men than me,' a line that would break your heart if he weren't so busy defending himself. It doesn't have the emotional reach of the quieter cuts in his catalog, and the joke, brilliant as it is, only unfolds the one way; this is a comic set piece, not a grower you'll return to for the ache. But as craft it's ruthless, the doo-wop chorus turning martial by the second pass, the whole thing cutting dead into silence before the muttered exit. Proof Rose can build a fully load-bearing song out of the least dignified subject he's ever touched.

### [6] message-from-bob  _standalone 4_

The comic run keeps going but the theology deepens here, and the joke stops being a joke somewhere around the second verse. Rose lets his brother's voicemails do the philosophizing over a dry, clipped Rhodes-and-bass vamp that stays close to a deadpan mid-tempo lope, laying out his premise like a proof: 'A message from Bob / is a message from God / if everything is one. / That's not religion. / That's arithmetic.' The band works in a punchy 1970s studio-jazz frame, fuzz guitar answering back after each refrain, the rhythm section snapping shut on the closing lines so the silence lands like a held breath. The bridge is the centerpiece, a flat catalog of household omens ('The refrigerator starts at two thirteen. / Information') that builds to a quiet thesis: 'Meaning is everywhere / once you lower the standard.' It runs long at five and a half minutes, and the last solo pass tests your patience the way any good koan does, but the payoff is the glued-shut window and the phone ringing once before the hard cut, where the whole conceit finally bares its teeth. Closer to Zappa's studio-jazz talking pieces than anything on his diarist records, this is Rose trusting you to sit with a strange idea, and it primes you for the two most ambitious things the record has left.

### [7] waltzing-tilde  _standalone 2_

After a long stretch of comic set-pieces, this is where the record turns most serious about its craft, and the joke goes fully philosophical. Two voices, a tenor and an alto, trade near-spoken lines over a waltz that keeps threatening to fall apart: piano and marimba lock into a three-note figure at the top, then the marimba quietly refuses to change while everything above it drifts, splinters into 5/4, and finally staggers through a few bars of 7/8 where the meter nearly loses its footing entirely. The whole thing is an argument about sameness that keeps dissolving under its own logic. 'We have the same car,' he offers; 'we don't have the same car,' she answers, and by the closing verse the only thing left that's identical is the pronoun. It's structurally the most ambitious thing in his catalog since 'Outlandia,' and if the payoff lives in the design more than in any single hook, that's the point: the marimba holding one wrong note under a full-ensemble unison is worth the wait. A grower, and the clearest signal that the closer is going to ask you to think rather than to sing along.

### [8] wazoo-in-effigy  _standalone 4_

There's a reason this one closes the record instead of opening it: it's an argument you have to sit inside, and after seven tracks of Rose sharpening his satire, the title cut turns the whole homage back on itself. The album tries and mostly succeeds as a tribute to Frank Zappa, but this is where Rose takes on Zappa's critics and asks why we haven't revisited that body of work in an age of mostly meaningless music as confection. The clipped baritone stays dry and unhurried through the first verse, all posture and no pity, 'Technique, certainly. Feeling? I doubt it,' delivered flat enough that you don't clock the trap until the chorus turns the accusation back around: 'I wrote what I heard. I said what I saw. You wanted approval; I wouldn't applaud.' At a brisk clip in a major key, the marimba pulse and off-kilter brass keep the room off balance, and the abrupt stop-start edits work like a critic's clipped sentences: everything measured, everything cut short before it can bleed. The masterstroke is structural. The spoken bridge, where the 'wild side hero' calls the thinly disguised Wazoo 'the single most untalented person I've heard in my life,' only to induct him into the Hall twenty years later with no correction printed, is the whole thesis in miniature, and it lands with the cold Randy Newman trick of letting a monster narrate his own portrait. It's not the easiest cut in the catalog, more built than felt, and the final bored 'Talentless' aftertaste is deliberate; it leaves you cold on purpose. A grower the faithful will keep coming back to unpack, this is Rose at his most quietly vicious, and it earns the last word. Say what you will about Zappa, but eventually genius gets recognized. Rose aims a small floodlight and asks for a reevaluation.

There's a reason this one closes the record instead of opening it: it's an argument you have to sit inside, and after seven tracks of Rose sharpening his satire, the title cut turns the whole homage back on itself. The album tries and mostly succeeds as a tribute to Frank Zappa, but this is where Rose takes on Zappa's critics and asks why we haven't revisited that body of work in an age of mostly meaningless music as confection. The clipped baritone stays dry and unhurried through the first verse, all posture and no pity, 'Technique, certainly. Feeling? I doubt it,' delivered flat enough that you don't clock the trap until the chorus turns the accusation back around: 'I wrote what I heard. I said what I saw. You wanted approval; I wouldn't applaud.' At a brisk clip in a major key, the marimba pulse and off-kilter brass keep the room off balance, and the abrupt stop-start edits work like a critic's clipped sentences: everything measured, everything cut short before it can bleed. The masterstroke is structural. The spoken bridge, where the 'wild side hero' calls the thinly disguised Wazoo 'the single most untalented person I've heard in my life,' only to induct him into the Hall twenty years later with no correction printed, is the whole thesis in miniature, and it lands with the cold Randy Newman trick of letting a monster narrate his own portrait. It's not the easiest cut in the catalog, more built than felt, and the final bored 'Talentless' aftertaste is deliberate; it leaves you cold on purpose. A grower the faithful will keep coming back to unpack, this is Rose at his most quietly vicious, and it earns the last word. Say what you will about Zappa, but eventually genius gets recognized. Rose aims a small floodlight and asks for a reevaluation.

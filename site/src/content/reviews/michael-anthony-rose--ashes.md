That hand drum under "Puerto Rico" swells into this: after twenty tracks of villains, machinery and moral vacuums, the album finally hands the microphone to the people the whole thing was done to, and it is a shock of warmth. It starts the way these things start: one voice, one cuatro, a woman who keeps a key to a house that lost half its roof. Rose builds "Ashes" the only honest way for a song about survival, from the room outward, letting adult voices, then children, then a full neighborhood choir fill in around that first elder until the chorus lands as a collective vow: "We remember each other / That is what a country's for." At an unhurried pace in A major the plena pulse never rushes; the panderetas and handclaps arrive like people showing up, and the bridge stripping down to voices and claps for the call-and-response ("Peace for whom?" "By whose hand?") is the sharpest piece of arrangement on the record, defiance carried on breath alone. What makes the track more than a rousing sing-along is the nerve of that ending: the song cuts off mid-phrase, "that is what a country's," a power-off drop, silence, and then a chirping state bulletin announcing "there were no survivors" before a game-show bumper mocks the whole thing into static, the regime's machinery breaking back in one last time. It is a gamble, and the tonal whiplash will send some listeners back to the choir rather than the joke; the bit runs a beat long. But the fury underneath is earned, and the human core, the spare house key, the cookbook birthdays by the stove, the neighbor who keeps the medicine when shipments do not land, holds every gram of it. A highlight, and one that lingers, made larger by everything cold that came before it.

After the loud television cruelty of the last stretch, the album drops its voice to a murmur, and it's more unnerving for it. This is Rose's morality play, and he casts it as a duet: Donin, the calm bass-baritone tactician, schooling an eager pupil in the art of winning without truth. In G minor, the arrangement keeps its powder dry, low organ, an upright pulse, a tremolo guitar that flickers at the edges, so the words carry the menace. And they do. The pre-chorus lands the whole thesis in four lines: "A fact arrives with documents / Dates and names and evidence / Truth arrives with appetite / It only needs a side to fight." That's the kind of couplet Randy Newman built careers on, a monster who talks in perfect logic. The chorus widens without ever raising its voice, "Aim what they believe / And let them guard it in the dark," and the escalation from "Facts can lose a hearing" to "Facts can lose a country" by the final round is the arc doing its quiet, chilling work. This also introduces Donin, the strategist whose calm will shadow the album's back half. The stripped-down bridge, "Loyalty over character / Retaliation over law," reads like a creed carved in stone. At five and a half minutes it asks for patience, and the recitative sections lean more theatrical than tuneful, so this rewards the second sit more than the first. But the closing exchange, "How do I begin" answered with "Leave the microphone on," is a gut-punch of a curtain line, and it hands the very next track its title. A highlight, and one of the most ambitious things here.

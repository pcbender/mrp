After a run of pure character farce, Rose lets the joke turn into something you can actually believe in, and this is the album's high-water mark. He has always found a lifetime in the ordinary, but here he goes further: he builds a whole theology out of an old friend's rambling voicemails. Over a dry, syncopated Rhodes-and-bass vamp that never quite settles, at a steady clip in a bright major key, the deadpan spoken vocal delivers Bob's misdialed apologies and glued-shut windows as if they were scripture, then the band snaps into a harmonized hook: "A message from Bob / is a message from God / if everything is one. / That's not religion. / That's arithmetic." The joke keeps sharpening into something closer to a real argument, and the bridge's inventory of red lights and falling ice cubes lands the thesis cold: "Meaning is everywhere / once you lower the standard." A single telephone ring near the five-minute mark triggers a hard cut that's the best-placed silence on the record since "You Don't Say," and the fuzz-guitar answers between verses keep the whole thing from tipping into pure sketch. It runs long, and the fourth verse leans more on concept than melody, but this is Rose at his most gleefully committed to a bit that turns out to mean it. Where the comedy stops being a costume and becomes a conviction. Play this one first.
